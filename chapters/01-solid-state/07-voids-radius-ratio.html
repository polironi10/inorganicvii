Voids and the radius ratio rule

Chapter 1 · Solid state and crystal structures

The empty spaces left between packed spheres are called interstitial voids. Two types matter.

VoidSurrounded byPer sphereLargest sphere that fits
TetrahedralFour spheres at the corners of a tetrahedron20.225 R
OctahedralSix spheres at the corners of an octahedron10.414 R

R is the radius of the packed sphere. An fcc unit cell has 4 atoms, so it has 8 tetrahedral voids and 4 octahedral voids.

Radius ratio rule

The radius ratio r+/r− predicts which hole a cation occupies, and therefore its coordination number. The limits are the ratios at which the cation just touches the surrounding anions.

r+/r−CoordinationGeometryExample
0.225 to 0.4144TetrahedralZnS
0.414 to 0.7326OctahedralNaCl
0.732 to 1.0008CubicCsCl

Worked limits, as written in the notes

CsCl, 8:8. Side of cube a = 2r−. Body diagonal √3 a = 2r+ + 2r−. Hence √3 = (r+ + r−)/r−, so r+/r− = √3 − 1 = 1.732 − 1 = 0.732.

NaCl, octahedral, 6:6. Face diagonal √2 a = 2r+ + 2r−, with a = 2r−. Hence r+/r− = √2 − 1 = 0.414.

ZnS, tetrahedral, 4:4. Half the body diagonal gives r+ + r− = √3 a/2, with the face diagonal √2 a = 2r−. The limiting ratio is 0.225.

Checked, with one correction to the working. The three limiting values, 0.225, 0.414 and 0.732, are correct and match the standard radius-ratio table. The intermediate algebra on the tetrahedral page of the notes is heavily crossed out and does not cleanly give 0.225 as written, so this page shows the standard result rather than reproducing that crossed-out line. The radius-ratio rule is a guide, not a law: it predicts only about half of the alkali halide structures correctly.
