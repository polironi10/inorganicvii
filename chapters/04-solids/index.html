The crystal and its lattice

Crystalline

  • Definite, repeating, long range order of particles.
  • Definite characteristic shape.
  • Sharp and fixed melting point.
  • Breaks along a definite plane, giving smooth surfaces.
  • A definite value of heat of fusion.

Examples: NaCl, diamond.

Amorphous

  • Irregular; only short range order.
  • Irregular shape.
  • Softens gradually over a range of temperature.
  • Breaks irregularly.
  • No definite value of heat of fusion.

Examples: glass, rubber, pitch.

Lattice and unit cells

A crystal consists of a basic structural unit, or motif, repeated in three dimensions. The basic unit may be a single atom, molecule or ion, or a group of atoms or molecules, which has an identical environment throughout the crystal. It is also called the basis of the crystal. A basis has the same stoichiometric composition as the crystal itself.

The lattice, or space lattice, is the geometrical idealisation of the structure. It is obtained by placing a point at the same location of each basis in the crystal; the resulting three-dimensional distribution of points in space forms the lattice. A crystal structure is generated by associating each lattice point with a basis.

lattice + basis = crystal structure

A unit cell is the smallest part of the crystal which can generate the whole crystal structure through repetition in three dimensions by simple translational displacement. In effect this is equivalent to constructing the whole crystal structure by stacking the unit cells together along their faces without leaving any space.

Lattice parameters

A unit cell is described by six numbers: a, b, c, the lengths of the three edges, and α, β, γ, the angles between the edges (α between b and c, β between a and c, γ between a and b).

Symmetry elements

A symmetry operation is a movement of a crystal after which it looks exactly the same as before. The imaginary point, line or plane about which the operation is carried out is called a symmetry element.

  1. Proper rotation axes (n-fold). Rotation by 360°/n about an axis leaves the crystal unchanged. Only 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6-fold axes are possible in a periodic crystal. A 5-fold axis (and 7, 8-fold) can never exist in a crystal, because space cannot be filled by repeating a pentagon without leaving gaps.
  2. Plane of symmetry (m). An imaginary plane that divides the crystal into two halves, each a mirror image of the other.
  3. Centre of symmetry (i). A point such that any line drawn through it meets the crystal at equal distances on both sides. Also called a centre of inversion.
  4. Roto-inversion axes (Sn). Combined rotation followed by inversion through a point. These are improper symmetry operations.

Combining all possible symmetry elements gives 32 point groups, which fall into the seven crystal systems. Depending on the symmetry of the unit cell crystal may be classified into 7 crystal system represented by 7 primitive unit cell.

The lattice counts add to 14 Bravais lattices in total: 3 + 2 + 4 + 2 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 14.

In a simple or primitive cubic lattice the atoms are present at all eight corners of the unit cell. Each corner is shared by eight adjacent cubic cells, so each corner contributes 1/8 of an atom.

8 corners × 1/8 = 1 atom per unit cell

In a body-centred cubic lattice the atoms are at all the corners and at the centre of the unit cell. The centre atom is not shared by any other cell.

8 × 1/8 + 1 = 2 atoms per unit cell

In a face-centred cubic lattice the atoms are at all eight corners and at the centre of each face. There is no atom at the centre of the cube. Each face-centre atom is shared by two adjacent cells, so it contributes 1/2.

Corners: 8 × 1/8 = 1
Faces: 6 × 1/2 = 3
Total = 4 atoms per unit cell

Close packing, voids and the radius ratio

Close packing in one dimension

Spheres are placed in a row, each touching two neighbours. Coordination number = 2.

Close packing in two dimensions

Square packing (AAA type). Rows are placed one above the other so that spheres sit directly on top of each other. It is less efficient. Coordination number = 4.

Hexagonal packing (ABAB type). The second row sits in the depressions (holes) of the first row. High efficient, this is the closest packing in two dimensions. Each sphere touches six neighbours. Coordination number = 6.

To build a crystal in three dimensions, close-packed layers are stacked one on top of the other in a regular way. Consider a sphere S in the first layer: spheres in the next layer nestle in the notches at the positions marked with dots. Three of these will make contact with S. In the second layer there is a notch over S, but there are also notches over the positions marked with crosses. The third layer may repeat the arrangement of the first layer, or it may not.

Such a pattern of packing of spheres in three dimensions is referred to as hexagonal close packing. It is called hexagonal because a six-fold (C6) axis runs perpendicular to the plane of the close-packed layer, present in all the stacked layers.

  • If the third layer repeats the first, there are only two kinds of layer, A and B, and the pattern is ABAB… This is the hexagonal close-packed (hcp) structure.
  • If the third layer does not repeat the first, it is denoted C, and the pattern is ABCABC… This is the cubic close-packed (ccp) structure, also called face-centred cubic (fcc).

Hexagonal close packing

This is the relation between the simple hexagonal crystal system and the hexagonal close-packing model. In ABA stacking, the seven spheres in both the first layer (A) and the third layer occupy simple hexagonal lattice points, while the three spheres of the second layer (B) are placed (within the unit cell) at the corners of an equilateral triangle midway between the two parallel hexagonal planes, so that the nearest environment of every sphere becomes identical. The height of the unit cell, c, is the distance between the first and third layers; a is the length of the side of the hexagon.

A sphere is hexagonally surrounded by six spheres in its own plane, and touches three spheres below the plane and three above it. Coordination number = 6 + 3 + 3 = 12.

Packing efficiency 74%, coordination number 12, stacking ABAB and so on.

Cubic close packing

In ccp / fcc the stacking sequence is ABCABC… The structure has cubic symmetry. In addition to the eight spheres at the eight corners of the cube, there is one sphere at the centre of each of the six faces, hence the name face-centred cubic. Each sphere is surrounded by 12 other nearest spheres, so the coordination number is 12. Packing efficiency is 74%.

Common features of hcp and ccp

  • Coordination number is 12 in both.
  • Packing efficiency is 74% in both, 74% of the total volume of the unit cell is occupied by the spheres.

Body-centred cubic, for comparison

Another common arrangement in a few metals is body-centred cubic (bcc). The second layer sits in the notches of the first and the pattern repeats ABAB…, but the coordination number is 8, compared with 12 in a close-packed pattern.

A line to read with care. The notes say, in the bcc paragraph, that the number of neighbours around any sphere is four. That is the in-plane count in the drawing, not the coordination number of bcc. The coordination number of bcc is 8, as the notes themselves state in the next sentence.

Radius ratio rule

The radius ratio r+/r− predicts which hole a cation occupies, and therefore its coordination number. The limits are the ratios at which the cation just touches the surrounding anions.

Worked limits

CsCl, 8:8. Side of cube a = 2r−. Body diagonal √3 a = 2r+ + 2r−. Hence √3 = (r+ + r−)/r−, so r+/r− = √3 − 1 = 1.732 − 1 = 0.732.

NaCl, octahedral, 6:6. Face diagonal √2 a = 2r+ + 2r−, with a = 2r− (a here is the side of the square of anions around the hole). Hence r+/r− = √2 − 1 = 0.414.

ZnS, tetrahedral, 4:4. A quarter of the body diagonal gives r+ + r− = √3 a/4, with the face diagonal √2 a = 4r−. Hence r+/r− = √6/2 − 1 = 0.225.

Diamond and ionic crystal structures

Diamond cubic unit cell

Diamond is a special type of fcc lattice. Four additional atoms are present within the face-centred cube, distributed symmetrically so that each one has an identical environment. They sit at the centres of alternating small cubes within the fcc cell, that is, at 4 of the 8 alternative tetrahedral holes. In this arrangement every atom is tetrahedrally surrounded.

Total lattice points per diamond cubic unit cell:

n = 1/8 × Nc + 1/2 × Nf + Ni = 1/8 × 8 + 1/2 × 6 + 4 = 1 + 3 + 4 = 8

The four internal lattice points lie along the four body diagonals of the fcc unit cell, each one a quarter of the way along a body diagonal from a corner. Taking a corner atom and the nearest atom along the body diagonal:

2r = 1/4 × √3 a
a = 8r / √3

Percentage of space actually occupied:

[8 × (4/3)πr³ × 100] / [(8/√3 r)³] = 34%

Space occupied 34%, space unoccupied 66%, 8 atoms per unit cell, coordination number 4.

Compare fcc metals at 74%: diamond is much more open, because every atom is held in a directional tetrahedral network rather than in close packing.

Zinc blende and wurtzite have the same 4:4 coordination and the same stoichiometry; they differ in the stacking of the anion array, ccp in zinc blende, hcp in wurtzite.

Fluorite, CaF2, 8:4

Ca2+ ions are arranged at the corners and at the centres of the faces of a cubic unit cell, and F− ions are at the centres of the eight small cubes into which the cell may be divided. Each Ca2+ is coordinated by 8 F− neighbours at the corners of a cube, while each F− has 4 Ca2+ neighbours.

This is the only structure in the notes with 8:4 coordination. The reverse structure, antifluorite, is taken by oxides such as Li2O and Na2O, with the cation and anion positions exchanged. (Standard material added here; it is not in the notes.)

Rutile, TiO2, 6:3

The unit cell of the rutile lattice is a given figure. The cation is octahedrally surrounded by six anions, while the anion (O2−) is trigonally surrounded by three cations. Rutile has a low-symmetry anion sublattice that fits neither a simple cubic nor an fcc lattice. The cation sublattice is a distorted body-centred cube, because one side of the cube differs from the others, the cation sublattice is actually body-centred tetragonal. The unit cell is tetragonal.

Perovskite, ilmenite, tolerance factor and spinel

Perovskite, CaTiO3

Named after the mineral CaTiO3. O2− ions, along with the larger cation (Ca2+), form a ccp and fcc array in which the octahedral holes formed by O2− are occupied by the smaller cation (Ti4+). In the unit cell, the eight corners of the cube are occupied by Ca2+, O2− are placed at the centres of the six faces, and Ti4+ is placed at the centre of the cube.

Ca2+: 8 × 1/8 = 1
O2−: 6 × 1/2 = 3
Ti4+: 1
CaTiO3

The structure can also be interpreted in terms of ReO3: the Re positions are occupied by Ti4+, while Ca2+ occupies the centre of the cube. Ti4+ is octahedrally surrounded by O2−, while Ca2+ is surrounded by 12 O2− ions residing at the centre of the edges of the cube.

The Goldschmidt tolerance factor (t) is a dimensionless number used to predict the stability of a crystal and its symmetry in ABO3 perovskites. It relates the ionic radii of the A-site cation (rA), the B-site cation (rB) and the anion (rO).

t = (rA + rO) / [ √2 (rB + rO) ]

When t deviates from the ideal value 1, the crystal lattice experiences a steric mismatch, driving structural distortion to lower-symmetry phases.

The notes give collapse below t = 0.8 or above t = 1.05, with MgTiO3 (t = 0.81 in the notes) as a non-perovskite example. CaTiO3 itself is orthorhombic, not cubic, at room temperature, despite t ≈ 0.97.

Structure of mixed metal oxides.

Normal spinel

Named after the mineral spinel, MgAl2O4. It consists of a ccp array of oxide ions in which the divalent metal ion (Mg2+) occupies 1/8 of the tetrahedral holes and the trivalent metal ion (Al3+) occupies 1/2 of the octahedral holes. In a ccp anion sublattice there is one octahedral hole and two tetrahedral holes per anion. There are four oxides per formula unit, and consequently four octahedral holes and eight tetrahedral holes.

Normal: (AII)tet (BIII)2oct O4

Inverse spinel

In an inverse spinel, half of the B ions occupy 1/8 of the tetrahedral holes, and A and the other half of B occupy 1/2 of the octahedral holes in the ccp array of O2−. In an inverse spinel the A2+ ion has a strong tendency to occupy octahedral holes, which is why the B3+ ion is displaced into the tetrahedral hole.

Inverse: BIIItet [AII BIII]oct O4

Why CFSE decides it

For transition-metal oxide spinels, O2− is a weak-field ligand, so the ions are usually high spin. The structure that forms is the one with the larger total CFSE. The octahedral site preference energy (OSPE) is CFSE(oct) − CFSE(tet), with Δt = 4/9 Δo. Its size depends on the d-electron count:

d count (high spin)CFSE oct (Δo)CFSE tet (Δo)OSPE (Δo)
d0, d5, d10000
d1, d60.400.270.13
d2, d70.800.530.27
d3, d81.200.360.84
d4, d90.600.180.42

So d3 and d8 have the strongest octahedral preference, d0, d5 and d10 have none, and the rest fall in between.

Point defects

There are different types of imperfection, such as point, surface and volume imperfections. Point defects are zero-dimensional, occurring at a single lattice site; extended defects may be one, two or three-dimensional (for example a stacking fault). Defects can also be classified as intrinsic and extrinsic.

Point defects in ionic crystals fall into two groups: stoichiometric defects (Schottky and Frenkel) and non-stoichiometric defects. Intrinsic defects (Schottky and Frenkel defects) occur in the pure crystal, while extrinsic defects originate from impurities present in the crystal. Defects can largely influence physical properties such as mechanical strength, electrical conductivity, colour and chemical reactivity. In ionic crystals only point imperfections are important.

Point imperfections

Point imperfections are very often referred to as zero-dimensional imperfections. They are localised in point-like regions and may arise due to:

  1. A vacancy in a lattice site.
  2. A substitutional impurity.
  3. Occurrence of a constituent ion in an interstitial space.
  4. Non-stoichiometric proportions of the constituent ions.
